Visual Thinking Strategies

October 26, 2026

The article made me think more about how quickly I usually look at an image and decide what it means. I liked the idea of slowing down, noticing smaller details, and asking what evidence in the image supports an interpretation. That feels especially relevant to design because an image can communicate different things depending on what someone notices first or what experiences they bring to it.

While looking through Awwwards, I found The Hands That Sew, an interactive website about the people who worked in the Balenciaga fashion houses. I thought one of the strongest parts of the site was how it combines photographs, stories, and interaction instead of presenting everything as a basic image gallery. Exploring the site gradually reveals more information about the people behind the clothing, which makes the images feel more personal and meaningful. One possible drawback is that the minimal and immersive design requires more exploration before it is immediately obvious where all of the information is located. Still, I think that works well with the subject because it encourages the viewer to slow down and discover information instead of seeing everything at once.

Overlays: Helpful or Distracting?

October 15, 2026

After reading Kathryn Whitenton’s Overuse of Overlays: How to Avoid Misusing Lightboxes, I realized overlays can easily become more annoying than helpful when used without a clear purpose. I had mostly thought of overlays as a useful way to show extra information without making someone leave the page. The article made me think more about situations where they actually make things harder, especially when the overlay has too much text, requires a lot of scrolling, or is difficult to close on a smaller screen. I also liked the idea of asking who, what, when, where, and why before deciding to use an overlay.

One point that stood out to me was that overlays should usually appear because of something the user chooses to do, rather than suddenly interrupting them. The article also recommends having a clear close option and allowing keyboard users to close the overlay with the Escape key. That connects directly to the overlay we made in class, since ours opens after clicking a link and can be closed with either the close button or Escape. Going forward, I would keep overlays short and focused. If the content needs a lot of scrolling or navigation, I think putting it on its own page would probably be easier for the user.

Form Design Best Practices

October 6, 2026

One thing that stood out to me was how much a form can improve just by removing unnecessary questions. I usually notice forms more when they are annoying to fill out, especially when they ask for information that does not seem necessary. The article also talked about using a single-column layout, which makes sense because it gives you one clear path down the page instead of making you figure out where to look next. Both ideas seem simple, but they can make a form feel much quicker and less overwhelming.

When I design forms myself, I would try to keep them simple and only ask for information that is actually needed. I would also make sure the labels and instructions are clear so people do not have to guess what they are supposed to enter. I think a good form should feel easy enough that you do not really have to think about how to use it. If I have to stop and figure out what a field means or where to go next, the design is probably making the process harder than it needs to be.
